when buddhists borrowed the wine god

June 2026

Somewhere in what is now Pakistan, in the 2nd or 3rd century CE, a sculptor carved a scene: a bearded figure holding a wine cup, surrounded by women, while nearby two men trample grapes and another drinks from a leather wine skin.

The scene could have come straight from a Roman sarcophagus. The costumes are Greek. The wine cups are kantharoi. The composition echoes Mediterranean banquet imagery stretching back centuries.

But this relief was not made for a Roman patron. It was carved for a Buddhist monastery in Gandhāra.

the visual vocabulary of pleasure

Gandhāra — the ancient region spanning modern Pakistan and Afghanistan — sat at a crossroads. Alexander's campaigns had passed through centuries earlier. The Silk Road channeled goods, ideas, and craftsmen between Rome and the East. Buddhist monasteries flourished under Kushan rule.

And in this meeting point, sculptors borrowed freely.

Relief panels from Gandhāran monasteries show Dionysus and his followers — satyrs, maenads, Silenus, Pan — engaged in the pleasures associated with the wine god: drinking, dancing, making music, making love. The figures wear Greek costume. The vine scrolls follow Roman patterns. Even the narrative structure mirrors Mediterranean models.

Yet these panels decorated Buddhist sacred spaces.

what traveled, what stayed behind

In Greek and Roman art, Dionysiac imagery carried religious weight. The god's myths told of death and rebirth. His cult promised ecstatic union with the divine. Wine was not merely pleasurable — it was a vehicle for transcendence.

When this visual vocabulary reached Gandhāra, something shifted.

The Gandhāran sculptors kept the forms: the cantharus cup, the vine wreath, the reclining banquet, the amorous couples. But they stripped away the religious significance. What remained was imagery of sensual pleasure — drinking, feasting, fraternizing — now repurposed to visualize the pleasures awaiting the faithful in the Buddhist afterlife.

The wine god became a picture of paradise.

how images travel

The transmission routes are well documented. Roman luxury goods — silver vessels, bronze cups, carved gems — traveled east along the Silk Road and by sea through the Indian Ocean. Ships from the Roman Empire docked at Barbarikon, at the mouth of the Indus, and their cargo moved upriver toward Gandhāra.

Plaster casts of metalwork designs were found at Begram, in Afghanistan — templates for local craftsmen. Terracotta molds for making such casts have been found in Gandhāra itself. Some scholars believe Roman artisans traveled east with the trade goods, bringing techniques and patterns directly.

A gilt silver plate found in Gansu Province, China, shows Dionysus riding a panther — the image traveling even further east, the wine god's form persistent even as his meaning dissolved.

the portable vine

Wine imagery has always been portable. The vine traveled from the Caucasus to the Mediterranean, from Rome to the edges of empire, from the Mediterranean to Central Asia and beyond. Everywhere it went, it carried meanings — fertility, transformation, pleasure, the divine — that local cultures absorbed, rejected, or transformed.

In Gandhāra, Buddhist patrons and sculptors looked at the visual language of Dionysus and saw something useful: a ready-made vocabulary for depicting earthly pleasures. The banquet, the cup, the vine, the embrace. Images that needed no translation, even if their original religious charge did not survive the journey.

What the Gandhāran reliefs reveal is how visual culture works across borders. Forms persist. Meanings shift. A wine god becomes a vision of paradise. A sacred symbol becomes decorative shorthand for pleasure.

The vine keeps traveling. The meaning keeps changing. Only the image remains.

Based on research by Tadashi Tanabe, "The Dionysiac Imagery from the Mediterranean to Gandhāra" (Ancient Punjab, Vol. 8, 2020)
