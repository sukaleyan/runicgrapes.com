# Judith Leyster — The Party and Its End (DRAFT)

**Lead:** Judith Leyster painted the joy of drinking — and the skeleton that waits when the cup is empty.

---

In the background of a drinking scene, a skeleton holds an hourglass and a skull. The two men in the foreground don't seem to notice — or perhaps they've chosen not to. One raises his glass, the other his pipe. The party continues, but death has already entered the room.

This is *The Last Drop*, painted around 1639 by Judith Leyster. It belongs to a genre the Dutch called "merry companies" — scenes of drinking, music, and flirtation that flourished in Haarlem in the early seventeenth century. But Leyster's version carries something heavier beneath its surface. The revelry is real, but so is the warning.

> **Pullquote:** The skeleton was once painted over — hidden beneath a lamp for centuries, then restored to view.

At some point in the painting's history, someone decided the skeleton was too much. It was covered with a lamp, turning a moral allegory into a simple tavern scene. Only later restoration revealed what Leyster had intended all along: a vanitas image, where the pleasures of the moment are shadowed by the certainty of their end. The smoke from the pipe, the last drops in the glass, the sand running through the hourglass — all point the same direction.

---

## a woman in the guild

Judith Leyster was born in Haarlem in 1609, the daughter of a brewery owner — a detail that makes her affinity for drinking scenes feel almost fated. By the time she was eighteen, a local poet was already praising her talent. In 1633, she became the first woman admitted to the Haarlem Guild of St. Luke, the painters' guild that controlled who could sell art and take on students. She opened her own workshop and began training apprentices, an almost unheard-of achievement for a woman of her time.

Her style drew from Frans Hals, the great portraitist of Haarlem, though whether she formally studied with him remains uncertain. What is certain is that in 1635, she took him to court. One of her students had left her workshop after only a few days to join Hals instead. The guild ruled in her favor — a small but telling sign of her standing.

In 1636, she married the painter Jan Miense Molenaer. After that, her output dropped sharply. Whether this was by choice, circumstance, or the quiet absorption of her identity into her husband's career, we cannot know. What we do know is that after her death in 1660, her name began to disappear. Her paintings were sold under other names — most often Frans Hals. For over two centuries, she was nearly erased from history.

---

## the rediscovery

In 1893, a painting long attributed to Frans Hals — *The Happy Couple* — was examined more closely. Beneath a forged Hals signature, someone noticed a monogram: the letters JL, with a star. It was Leyster's signature, hidden in plain sight. The discovery led the Dutch art historian Cornelis Hofstede de Groot to investigate further, and within a few years, seven more works were reattributed to her. Today, around thirty-five paintings bear her name — a small body of work, but one that speaks clearly.

---

## the merry company

Leyster's earliest dated works already show her fascination with the genre. *The Jolly Drinker*, painted in 1629, captures a man mid-laugh, his feathered cap askew, his cheeks flushed, lifting an open beer jug toward the viewer as if inviting us to join him. There is no skeleton here, no warning — only the immediate pleasure of the moment, rendered with a warmth and directness that draws you in.

A year later, she painted *The Merry Company*: a young couple making music and drinking together. The woman pours from a pitcher while sending a half-smile toward her companion; he plays a violin and looks out at us, relaxed and knowing. The atmosphere is intimate, slightly mischievous, charged with an undercurrent of flirtation. The drink flows, the music plays, and the world outside the frame doesn't matter.

Two-thirds of Leyster's genre paintings involve drinking, smoking, gaming, or music — the full repertoire of merry company subjects. She understood the appeal of these scenes: the looseness of a good night, the glow of candlelight on flushed faces, the way drink opens a room. But she also understood what came after.

---

## from joy to warning

Ten years separate *The Jolly Drinker* from *The Last Drop*. In that decade, something shifted. The pure celebration of the earlier work gives way to something more complex — not a rejection of pleasure, but an acknowledgment of its limits. The men in *The Last Drop* are still drinking, still smoking, still caught in the moment. But the skeleton behind them refuses to let us forget what they've chosen to ignore.

> **Blockquote:** The smoke rises, the glass empties, the hourglass runs. Every symbol in the painting points the same way.

This kind of image — where enjoyment and mortality share the same frame — was common in Dutch art of the period. The vanitas tradition reminded viewers that earthly pleasures were fleeting, that youth and beauty would fade, that the only certainty was the end. Drink, with its associations of both celebration and excess, was a natural subject for this kind of meditation. You could paint the party and the warning in a single scene.

But in Leyster's hands, the warning feels personal. She had painted the joy. She knew its texture, its warmth, its seduction. And now she painted its shadow — not to condemn, but to complete the picture. The party is real. So is its end.

Perhaps that's why someone once tried to hide the skeleton. The truth it carried was too direct, too uncomfortable for a simple tavern scene. But Leyster had put it there for a reason. And now, centuries later, it looks back at us again.
