the vine

A dance frozen in bronze, yet alive with movement.

June 2026

Harriet Whitney Frishmuth's The Vine is more than just a sculpture — it's a tribute to the rhythm of life itself.

Created in 1921, this bronze figure captures a dancer in full arc, her body curved backward in a moment of abandon. In her extended hand, she holds a grapevine — not as decoration, but as partner. The vine becomes an extension of her movement, curving with her, completing the gesture.

The vine weaves through her, grounding her while setting her spirit free.

inspired by dance

Frishmuth studied under Auguste Rodin in Paris and later worked extensively with professional dancers as her models. She was particularly influenced by the revolution in modern dance happening around her — a movement that rejected the rigid formality of ballet in favor of natural, expressive movement.

At the center of this revolution was Isadora Duncan, who famously danced barefoot, draped in flowing fabric, drawing inspiration from ancient Greek art. Duncan believed dance should express the soul's connection to nature — freedom, ecstasy, the body moving as it was meant to move.

Frishmuth absorbed this vision. Her sculptures don't just depict dancers — they embody the philosophy. The figures twist and reach, balanced on tiptoe, caught in moments that feel both fleeting and eternal.

a modern maenad

Look at The Vine long enough and older images begin to surface. The grapes draped across her body, the ecstatic pose, the sense of being overtaken by something larger than oneself — this is the iconography of the maenad.

In ancient Greece, maenads were the female followers of Dionysus, the wine god. They danced in his honor, crowned with ivy and vine leaves, lost in divine frenzy. Artists depicted them whirling, heads thrown back, holding thyrsoi (fennel staffs wrapped in ivy) or grapes.

Could this be a modern maenad, lost in a Bacchic trance?

Frishmuth never explicitly called her figure a maenad. But the connection runs deeper than intention. The vine is not incidental — it is identity. The dancer holds it not as prop but as partner, as if the boundary between woman and plant has begun to dissolve.

transformation, ecstasy, growth

The vine was no arbitrary choice. It carries millennia of symbolic weight: transformation (grape to wine), ecstasy (the Dionysiac rites), and the eternal cycle of growth, death, and return.

In Frishmuth's hands, these ancient meanings merge with modern ones. The dancer's freedom — her rejection of corsets and conventions, her insistence on moving as the body desires — becomes another kind of Dionysiac release. The vine grounds her in tradition while her movement breaks free of it.

Bronze is a strange medium for capturing movement. It is heavy, permanent, fixed. Yet The Vine feels weightless. The figure seems to hover on one toe, caught mid-breath, the metal somehow lighter than air.

That tension — between stillness and motion, weight and flight, ancient symbol and modern body — is what makes the sculpture endure. It is frozen, yes. But it is still dancing.
